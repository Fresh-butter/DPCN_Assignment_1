\section*{The problem this survey poses}

About 80\% of this class agrees with almost everything. Out of 5158 answers actually
given across 60 statements, 4127 are ``Agree'' or ``Strongly Agree'', and per-item
means sit between 3.5 and 4.5 on a five-point scale. If you just take the raw responses
and compute cosine similarity between students, everyone looks nearly identical
($\sim$0.96 for every pair), and you end up with a near-complete graph that tells you
nothing.

So the real question isn't what people answered. It's how far each answer sits
from what everyone else said. Once we centre each item on its mean, genuine structure
shows up, and there turns out to be quite a lot of it.

We built three networks from this one dataset. They use the same cleaned data but
differ in what counts as a node and what counts as a relationship, so they end up
answering different questions. The most interesting results come from putting them
side by side: where two of them agree, it's hard to dismiss as an artifact, and where
they disagree, the disagreement itself usually turns out to be interesting.

\section{Dataset documentation}

The raw file is $96 \times 61$: one respondent identifier and 60 Likert statements,
split into four blocks of fifteen, i.e., \textbf{T}echnology, \textbf{E}ducation,
\textbf{S}ociety/Ethics, and en\textbf{V}ironment. All cleaning is done once in
\texttt{common/contract.py} and imported by all three members, so nobody re-cleans
the data separately.

\textbf{Why 30.} There's a natural gap in the data. 68 rows have zero missing answers,
another 17 have 1--4, then one row each has 10 and 15. After that the count jumps
straight to 45 (four rows) and 60 (five rows). Nothing at all falls between 15 and 45.
Any threshold in that gap keeps exactly the same 87 respondents. The nine rows we
drop are basically abandoned responses, not partial ones.

\textbf{Why $z$-score.} This is what makes everything else possible.
Raw pairwise correlation between students averages $0.267$; after centring each item on
its own mean, pairwise-complete cosine similarity averages $-0.010$ and spreads across
$[-0.638, +0.758]$. The ceiling goes away and actual variation becomes visible.
We $z$-score over all 87 respondents so that the three members' values are on the
same reference frame.

\textbf{What we didn't standardise.} The 62 remaining missing cells are handled
differently by each construction, since that's really a modelling choice:
pairwise-complete cosine with $\geq$20 shared items (A),
pairwise-complete Pearson with \texttt{min\_periods=10} (B), and simply no edge for a
missing cell (C). Each member also ran a mean-imputed variant as a sanity check;
the differences are tiny (for B, mean $|\Delta r| = 0.0033$).

Two items, E02 and T10, looked like they might be reverse-worded. We flagged them but
didn't flip them. Since we have a signed network, it should be able to discover
reverse-coding as negative edges on its own.
E02 does end up behaving as a minority-opinion item; T10 does not.

Because all three members import the same loader, the three pipelines produce
bit-identical $87 \times 60$ matrices. This is what lets us do the cross-network
comparisons in Section 4: A and C describe the same 87 students, B and C the same 60
statements, so every comparison is one population measured two ways.

\textbf{A: agreement network.} Two students get an edge when their $z$-scored
response vectors are similar enough. A cosine threshold of $0.15$ gives 822 edges,
mean degree 18.9, and one connected component. This isn't a knife-edge choice:
sweeping the threshold, the giant component holds every node up to $0.225$ and only
drops below half past $0.325$. We also built a mutual-$k$NN version ($k=8$) as an
alternative, which we use for the spreading dynamics in Section 3.4.

\textbf{B: signed statement network.} Statements are connected by the Pearson
correlation of their response profiles across students, keeping the sign so that
opposition shows up as negative edges. We test significance against a permutation null
where each item is independently shuffled, with Benjamini--Hochberg FDR at $q=0.05$.
402 of 1770 candidate pairs survive (smallest retained $|r|$ is $0.274$). We first tried
a family-wise max-statistic threshold, but that demanded $|r| > 0.50$ and killed
every negative edge- which defeated the whole point of building a signed network.
The per-edge FDR rule keeps 391 positive and 11 negative edges.

\textbf{C: bipartite deviation network.} Instead of removing the ceiling effect and
studying what's left, this one makes the residual the actual object of study. A
student gets connected to a statement when they answered it more than one SD
from the class mean, weighted by $|z|$. At cutoff $1.0$ this gives 1200 edges, with
every student and every statement participating.

All these thresholds are somewhat arbitrary, i.e. a slightly different value would also
be defensible. So each one was swept. The short version: coarse structure doesn't change,
fine detail does. A's threshold and $k$NN rules share only 25.2\% of their edges by
Jaccard overlap but still give the same two communities, and C's cutoff range
$0.75$--$1.5$ swings the edge count from 2856 to 442 without changing any of the
conclusions below.

All results use \texttt{SEED = 42}. We also checked that re-running everything from
scratch produces identical outputs.

\section{Analysis and visualisations}

\subsection{The class splits into two blocs, and it's about intensity}

Louvain on the agreement network finds two main communities of 48 and 35
students (plus two tiny pairs) at modularity $Q = 0.432$. On its own that number
doesn't mean much. Louvain will find some partition on any graph. What matters is the
comparison to a degree-preserving configuration model, which scores
$0.178 \pm 0.007$ over 30 rewirings. The observed value is \textbf{35.5 standard
deviations above that}. The partition is also strongly assortative: 746 of 822 edges
(90.8\%) stay within a community, and within-community edges carry more weight than
between-community ones (0.286 vs 0.212).

The network is also much more transitive than you'd expect by chance. Average local
clustering is $0.505$ against an Erd\H{o}s--R\'enyi null of $0.220 \pm 0.009$, about
32 SD above. If a student agrees with two others, those two very likely agree
with each other too. Average shortest path length ($2.054$) is actually slightly
\textit{longer} than the null ($1.792$), so the network is locally cliquey rather than
small-world.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.7\textwidth]{../member_a_agreement_net/figures/fig04_network_communities.png}
\caption{The agreement network over 87 students, coloured by Louvain community. The two
main blocs are clearly visible and far from random
($Q = 0.432$ vs a configuration null of $0.178 \pm 0.007$).}
\end{figure}

The more interesting part is \textit{what} these two blocs actually differ on. They're
not opposed camps. Both rank the same statements highest; they just differ in how
strongly they agree. One bloc (35 students) has overall mean $z$ of $+0.371$, the
other (48 students) $-0.283$. It's a split in conviction, not in position. One group
feels strongly, the other is more moderate, but they're not on opposite sides.

And this intensity gap is concentrated in specific topics. Of the twenty statements that
best separate the blocs, eleven are Environment and seven are Society/Ethics; only one
each from Technology or Education. Mean absolute $z$-gap per item by block:
V $0.986$, S $0.835$, E $0.536$, T $0.434$.

\centering
\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{../member_a_agreement_net/figures/fig07_community_profile.png}
\caption{What separates the two blocs. \textit{Left:} the twenty most discriminating
statements, Eighteen of them are Environment or Society/Ethics. \textit{Right:} the same
result aggregated by block. The class divides over how strongly to hold environmental and
social positions, not over technology or education.}

\subsection{Disagreement is rare, but it's concentrated}

The signed statement network is overwhelmingly positive: 391 of 402 edges. The eleven
negative edges are the interesting ones, and they're not scattered randomly.
Eight of the eleven touch a single statement - E03 (``Class attendance should be compulsory
for all courses''), which anti-correlates with a cluster of
progressive-education and innovation items (E04, E07, E10, E11, E12, T06, S06). What
looks like random dissent is actually one clear axis: traditionalist vs
progressive views on pedagogy.

The network is also almost perfectly balanced. Out of 1298 closed
triads, \textbf{zero} are frustrated; every existing triangle has consistent signs.
The frustration index (minimum sign flips needed for a clean
two-faction split) is 2 out of 402 edges. A null model that reshuffles which edges carry
the eleven negative signs while keeping the topology fixed gives $10.98 \pm 0.16$, putting
the observed value 56 SD \textit{below} chance. (The two ``wrong'' edges must sit on a
longer signed cycle since the triangle census is already perfect.)
Basically, whatever this class believes, it believes it consistently. There are
almost no cases of holding contradictory positions.

One more thing: Louvain on the positive backbone
finds eight communities whose match with the survey's T/E/S/V blocks is weak (NMI $= 0.277$).
The class doesn't organise its opinions along the topic boundaries the survey designers
set up. The clearest example is T08 (AI-assisted diagnosis) and T09 (autonomous vehicles),
which survive the FDR cut with \textit{zero edges}. Two Technology statements that
don't correlate with anything in the survey, including the rest of Technology.

\subsection{Consensus has structure; dissent does not}

The bipartite network asks the opposite question, i.e., not who agrees, but who breaks
from the class and on what. The most deviated-on statement is T13 (cybersecurity investment),
where 60 of 87 students answered more than one SD from the mean. Interestingly,
the lowest-mean items are \textit{not} the highest-deviation items: E03 and E02, which
are central to B's disagreement structure, rank only eleventh and below here. A low mean
means the class agrees something is false; high deviation degree means the class
can't settle on it. Those are different things.

Projecting this bipartite graph onto the 87 students, i.e., connecting two students when they
deviate on the same issues, gives what was probably our most surprising result. Louvain returns
two communities at $Q = 0.0368$, which is low. But low isn't the same as meaningless, so we
compared it against a degree-preserving null, same as we did for network A. The null scores
$0.0567 \pm 0.0032$. So the co-dissent network isn't just weakly clustered, it's
\textbf{6.1 SD \textit{less} clustered than a random graph} with the same degree
sequence.

Compare that with A's $+35.5$ SD on the same 87 students, and you get the main finding:
when students are linked by what they agree on, they form two clear
camps. When the exact same students are linked by what they dissent on, there's
basically no structure at all. There's no ``contrarian clique'', disagreeing with the class on
one thing doesn't predict what else you'll disagree on.

Sorting the incidence matrix by degree does give a nested-looking, triangular pattern,
and NODF $= 41.8$ suggests a hierarchy of dissent, i.e. that students work
down a shared ordering of how contentious each statement is. But that doesn't hold up
against a null either.
NODF goes up automatically with heterogeneous degrees, and degrees here range from 3 to 35
deviations per student. Against a curveball null that fixes both degree sequences, the null
NODF is $41.00 \pm 0.26$: the observed value clears it by just $+0.79$ points out of
100 ($z = +3.08$). Technically above chance, but practically nothing.

\subsection{Running processes on these networks}

In a few places, running a dynamical process on the network showed things that the
static structure alone didn't.

\textbf{Random walk on the deviation network is all about degree.} A walker going
student $\to$ item $\to$ student can only travel between students through an issue they
both deviate on, so mean first-passage time (MFPT) works as an issue-mediated distance.
Computed exactly from the fundamental matrix with the true stationary distribution
$\pi_j \propto \mathrm{strength}_j$, MFPT ranges from $39.8$ to $1063.5$ steps, i.e. a 27-fold
spread that looks like it should mean something interesting. But it doesn't, really.
MFPT correlates with deviation degree at Spearman $\rho = -0.971$, and dividing out
node strength leaves a relative MFPT range of just $1.005$--$1.118$. The deviation
network is basically indistinguishable from a configuration model: once you know how
often a student deviates, you know pretty much everything about how the dynamics reach them.
This is the same thing the modularity and nestedness nulls told us, just from a
third angle.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{../member_c_bipartite_net/figures/fig04_mfpt_structure.png}
\caption{Mean first-passage time on the bipartite deviation network. \textit{Left:} raw
MFPT spans 27-fold, but tracks deviation degree at $\rho = -0.971$. \textit{Right:}
dividing out node strength leaves only 11\% residual spread.}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Seeding high-centrality students backfires on sparse graphs.} We ran SI spreading
from three seeds, 30 trials per condition, comparing top-eigenvector seeds against
random ones. On the dense threshold graph the choice doesn't matter: both reach
basically the whole class. On the sparser mutual-$k$NN graph, centrality seeding is
actually \textit{worse}: coverage at $t=30$ is $0.244 \pm 0.266$ for the top eigenvector
seeds vs $0.487 \pm 0.401$ for random (mean $\pm$ SD, 30 trials; Welch
$t = 2.76$, $p = 0.008$). Worth noting is that on a graph this sparse an outbreak either takes off
or dies out, so the SD is as large as the mean and individual trials range from
near-zero to near-complete coverage.

The reason is seed redundancy. Eigenvector centrality rewards being in the same dense
core, so the three top students are all right next to each other. Their
mean pairwise distance 1.00 hops, versus 3.33 for random seeds on the same graph.
They're basically covering the same area. On the dense graph where
everyone is about 2 hops from everyone else, that redundancy doesn't cost anything. But
make the graph sparser and it becomes the dominant effect.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=1.0\textwidth]{../member_a_agreement_net/figures/fig08_si_seeding_density.png}
\caption{SI spreading from three seeds, 30 trials per condition, shading $\pm 1$ SD.
\textit{Left:} on the dense threshold graph the seeding strategy makes no difference.
\textit{Right:} on the sparse mutual-$k$NN graph random seeding reaches roughly twice as
many students on average, though the bands overlap a lot; on a sparse graph
results are close to all-or-nothing.}
\end{figure}

We also ran Deffuant--Weisbuch bounded-confidence dynamics on the agreement network and
Altafini signed consensus on the statement network. Both behaved as expected. A
fragmentation-to-consensus transition around $\varepsilon \approx 0.30$--$0.55$,
and slow monotone decay from $1.21$ to $0.08$ (consistent with a nearly balanced
network). Neither gave us anything that distinguishes this class from any other,
so we just note them here briefly.

\section{Results and discussion}

\subsection{Two different constructions rank the same statements the same way}

Member A measures, for each statement, the gap in mean $z$-score between the two
student blocs, i.e. how much that statement separates the class. Member B measures
how far a perturbation to that statement propagates through the signed correlation
network, its dynamical leverage. These come from different graphs, different node sets,
and different math. Across the 58 statements both can score, they agree at
Spearman $\rho = +0.784$ ($p = 3.5 \times 10^{-13}$; Pearson $r = +0.617$).

Grouped by block, they give the same ordering: \textbf{V $>$ S $>$ E $>$ T}.

The spread is also worth looking at. Environment's leverage has an SD
of $0.014$, i.e. all fifteen V statements fall between $0.914$ and $0.967$. Technology's is
eighteen times larger at $0.249$, ranging from $0.233$ to $0.953$. Environment acts as
one tightly coupled bloc in the opinion graph; Technology is more of a loose collection of
statements that just happen to share a label. This lines up with B finding that the
class's communities don't match the survey's topic blocks, and with T08 and T09
not correlating with anything at all.

\subsection{Influence and deviation go in opposite directions}

The third column in that table runs backwards. Member C's deviation
degree, how many students answered a statement more than one SD from the mean,
anti-correlates with both other measures, i.e. $\rho = -0.390$ ($p = 0.002$) against B's
leverage, and $\rho = -0.349$ ($p = 0.006$) against A's bloc gap.

So the statements with the most influence and the sharpest class-dividing power are
the ones individuals deviate from \textit{least}. Environment is where the class is
most tightly coupled. V statements sort students into camps by
\textit{how strongly} they agree, while attracting almost no individual dissent.
Technology and Education are where people actually break ranks.

This also means there are two different senses of ``controversial'' here, and they point
in opposite directions. B finds E03 as the disagreement axis (8 of 11 negative
edges). C finds T13 as the biggest flashpoint (60 of 87 students deviating). Both
are correct. E03 produces \textit{patterned anti-correlation}, splitting the class into
opposed sides. While T13 produces \textit{raw spread}, where everyone answers
differently but with no particular pattern.

\subsection{Putting the three networks together}

The clearest overall result is the asymmetry between agreement and dissent.
On the same 87 students, agreement structure sits $35.5$ SD above its null while
co-dissent sits $6.1$ SD \textit{below} its own. Three independent measurements on
network C, modularity, nestedness, MFPT, all say the same thing, i.e. once you know
how often a student deviates, you know basically everything about where they sit in the
network.

That agreement and dissent really are measuring different things (not just two views of one
structure) is confirmed directly. Correlating A's pairwise similarity against C's
symmetrised issue-mediated distance across 3741 student pairs gives
$r = +0.036$ ($\rho = +0.026$), 0.1\% of variance explained. The $p$-value only
clears $0.05$ because 3741 pairs make tiny effects detectable; there's essentially no
relationship here. Knowing two students think alike tells you nothing about whether
they break from the class on the same questions.

Stepping back, the picture of this class is that a cohort that agrees on most things and
agrees consistently, clustering at 2.3$\times$ the random baseline, zero frustrated
triangles out of 1298. The real division isn't topical but about intensity, separating
those who feel strongly about environmental and social issues from those who hold
the same views more mildly. The disagreements that exist are concentrated on a
single pedagogical axis rather than spread across topics. And individual dissent is
genuinely idiosyncratic; students break from consensus at very different rates, but
\textit{which} issues they break on seems to be personal rather than factional.

\subsection{Limitations}

This is a single class of 87 students, so none of this generalises beyond it.
The ceiling effect, while handled, still limits what we can claim: after
$z$-scoring we're analysing residuals around a near-universal agreement, and the
``moderate'' bloc still agrees with most statements. Every network needed a threshold, and
while the overall structure is robust to all of them, individual edges aren't. A's two
edge rules share only a quarter of their edges. Claims about specific edges or individual
students should be taken with that in mind; claims about blocs and null comparisons hold
across the sweeps. Also, leverage saturates near $0.96$, so the $\rho = +0.784$ correlation
in Figure 8 is driven disproportionately by the lower tail.
